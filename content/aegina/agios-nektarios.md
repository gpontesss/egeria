## Why go

St Nektarios — Metropolitan of
Pentapolis, slandered out of his see in Egypt, who spent the last decades
of his life as a school chaplain in Athens and then as the founder of this
convent, and who was glorified in 1961 — is among the most widely venerated
saints of the modern Greek world, and the whole of him is here: his relics,
the tomb he was first buried in, the cell he died in, and the convent he
built with his own hands.

## What is there to venerate

Three distinct things, all on the one site, and pilgrims do all three:

**The relics**, in the old katholikon of the convent. His skull is kept in a
reliquary; the rest of his relics are in the church. When his grave was
opened some years after his burial his body was found incorrupt, and the
marble of the tomb and the cloths had taken on the fragrance that is still
the commonest thing pilgrims report here.

**The tomb**, in the courtyard — now empty, roofed, and open. The custom is
to put your head into the tomb opening and pray; the marble is worn from it.

**His cell**, preserved as it was: bed, desk, vestments, the few
possessions of a bishop who lived like a gardener. A small museum adjoins it.

The enormous modern basilica on the hillside above — one of the largest
churches in Greece — is dedicated to him but is not where his relics are.
Pilgrims often see only the big church and leave, having missed the point;
go up the steps to the old convent.

## History

St Nektarios restored a ruined and abandoned monastery on this site from
1904 onwards, settling a small community of nuns in it, and lived here
until his death in 1920 in the Aretaieio hospital in Athens. He was
glorified by the Ecumenical Patriarchate in 1961. The convent is still a
working house of nuns, which is worth remembering when the coaches arrive.

## Practical notes

Bus from Aegina town, or a ten-minute taxi. Get there early — by late
morning in season it is full of day-trip groups off the Piraeus boats, and
the old katholikon is small. The Liturgy here in the morning, before the
first boat arrives, is a different place altogether.

Dress is genuinely enforced at the gate. Oil and cotton wool for anointing,
and bottles for the holy water, are available on site.
