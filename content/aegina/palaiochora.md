## Why go

The medieval capital of Aegina, built inland and uphill in the ninth
century to be invisible from the sea during the Saracen raids, sacked by
Barbarossa in 1537, and finally abandoned in the 1820s when the modern town
on the coast was founded. The houses are gone. The churches were left
standing — more than thirty of them survive on the hillside, scattered
among the rocks, many with frescoes, several still roofed.

It is the densest concentration of Byzantine and post-Byzantine chapels
anywhere in the Saronic Gulf, and it is free, unguarded and usually empty.

## What is there

Among the churches identified on the site: the Episkopi (the cathedral of
the old town), the Taxiarches, St George the Catholic (so called from the
Venetian period), Sts Anargyroi, the Metamorphosis, St Nicholas, St
Dionysios, St Anna, and a long tail of smaller chapels, some of them little
more than a vaulted cell against the rock.

**St Dionysios of Zakynthos** lived on Aegina for a period in the
sixteenth-seventeenth century and served here; the chapel bearing his name
is one of the pilgrimage points on the hill. Which chapel it is, and what
the local tradition actually claims, is best had on the island — its own
account is more reliable than anything written at a distance.

## Practical notes

Walkable from the St Nektarios monastery — they adjoin, and the standard
visit does both together. Allow two hours on the hill to go up to the
Venetian castle at the top and look into the chapels on the way. No shade,
no water, no facilities. Go in the morning.
