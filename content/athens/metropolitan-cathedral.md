## Why go

The two relics that make Athens a pilgrimage are both here, in the same
building and a few metres apart: St Philothei of Athens, who was beaten to
death in 1589 for ransoming Greek women out of Ottoman harems, and the
skull of St Gregory V, the Patriarch of Constantinople hanged at the gate
of the Patriarchate on Pascha 1821 and thrown into the Bosphorus. They are the one thing in the city
not to miss.

## What is there to venerate

St Philothei's relics lie in a silver reliquary on the north side of the
nave. She is the patroness of the city, and her relics have been in Athens
continuously since her death: unusually for relics of her era, they were
never carried off, hidden abroad, or divided. The reliquary is
open for veneration.

The skull of St Gregory V is kept in its own shrine in the cathedral. His
body was recovered from the sea by Greek sailors, taken to Odessa, and
returned to Athens in 1871 for the fiftieth anniversary of the Revolution;
he was glorified in 1921.

## History

Built 1842–62 from the stone of some seventy demolished Byzantine churches
— the new kingdom's cathedral assembled, quite literally, out of the old
city's churches. Architecturally it is nineteenth-century and not
especially interesting. It is here for what it holds, not for what it is.

## Practical notes

The Little Metropolis (see below) stands in the same square, twenty paces
away, and the two are always visited together. Plaka's Byzantine churches —
Kapnikarea, Agioi Theodoroi, Panagia Pantanassa, the Holy Apostles in the
Agora — are all within a fifteen-minute walk.
