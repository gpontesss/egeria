## Why every entry names a jurisdiction

Because it changes what a visit is. A place in this gazetteer may be any of
the following, and the difference is practical before it is anything else:

- a **functioning Orthodox church or monastery**, where there are services,
  relics are brought out for veneration, and you are a guest in someone's
  house;
- a **state museum** — Daphni, Kaisariani, the Byzantine Museum — where
  there are no services and nothing in anyone's jurisdiction, only a
  building and a ticket;
- a **working mosque** that was once a church — Hagia Sophia, Chora, Little
  Hagia Sophia, Zeyrek — where you remove your shoes, cover your head, and
  come outside prayer times;
- an **open site or ruin** — the Areopagus, Palaiochora, Kalikatsou — with
  no custodian, no hours, and nothing between you and the stones.

The entries therefore name the jurisdiction as a plain fact: the Church of
Greece, the Ecumenical Patriarchate, the Bulgarian or Russian Church, one of
the Old Calendar synods, or the state. Knowing which before you arrive is the
difference between a visit you have thought about and one you have not.

**This gazetteer does not go further than that.** Whether and how a visitor
of one jurisdiction should venerate or commune in the church of another is a
question for a priest, not for a list of places, and nothing here attempts an
answer. The facts are given so that the question can be settled in advance
rather than in front of a reliquary.

## The calendar, and what it does to a date

Two reckonings are in use across the Orthodox world:

- the **Julian** ("Old") calendar, kept by the Church of Russia, Serbia,
  Georgia, Jerusalem, Mount Athos, and the Old Calendar churches of Greece,
  Romania and Bulgaria;
- the **Revised Julian** ("New") calendar, kept by the Ecumenical
  Patriarchate, the Church of Greece, Romania, Bulgaria, Antioch, Alexandria,
  and most of the diaspora.

For the **fixed** feasts the two currently fall **thirteen days apart**. For
**Pascha** and everything that moves with it, almost all Orthodox churches —
on either calendar — follow the same old Paschalion, so those dates coincide.

Two consequences for a visit:

**A patronal feast is kept on the host church's date.** If you want to be at
a place for its feast — St Nektarios on 9 November, the Three Hierarchs on
30 January, the Dormition on 15 August — find out which calendar that
particular church keeps, and go on *its* date. The dates in these entries are
given in the civil calendar as the host institution itself reckons them.

**Visiting hours move with the host's fasts and feasts too.** Monastery
opening contracts around Great Lent and around the house's own festal cycle,
which may not be the cycle you are keeping.

## A letter of introduction

Worth more than anything else on this page, and the one thing that cannot be
got from a website. For monasteries — and especially for any house where you
hope to be received rather than shown around — a letter from your own parish
priest opens doors that are closed to a stranger at the gate. Ask well before
you travel.
